## 09 · A Guide to Paper Figures

### 9.1　Overview

> **Tip**　A good figure should let a reviewer answer three questions fast: what important problem does this paper solve? Why are existing methods not good enough? What is your method's advantage, and how well does it work? Many reviewers start by scanning the title, the abstract, and the figures. So figure quality directly shapes the first impression, and a good figure lets readers grasp the core contribution within seconds.

Research figures in a paper fall into three groups:

- **Motivation figure (teaser)**: usually near the beginning, showing the paper's core motivation, a problem observation, or the method's advantage. Its job is to create interest fast, so readers feel "this problem really matters, and this paper's angle is interesting."
- **Method figure**: architecture diagrams, algorithm sketches, data-construction diagrams, etc.
- **Results figure**: result tables, bar charts, line plots, scatter plots, heatmaps, radar charts, case visualizations, etc.

For the first two, you can write a prompt and have AI draft a sketch, then rebuild it properly in PowerPoint or Visio and refine by hand. The third can be generated by a coding agent.

### 9.2　The Teaser Figure

Usually placed at the start of the paper or in the Introduction. A teaser only needs to carry two things:

- **Problem or observation**: what problem does the paper attack, or what is the limitation of existing methods, or what is a typical, representative, counter-intuitive failure case or observation?
- **Advantage**: what is the advantage of this paper's method?

Before drawing, settle one sentence:

> **Tip**　What should a reader remember after seeing this figure?

If you cannot state that sentence, you cannot draw the figure well.

Core principle: **Self-Contain** — a reader should understand the figure without flipping to the rest of the paper.

#### Color audit: this figure has almost no "color scheme", and that is its strength

| Dimension | What it does | Assessment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main color source | Entirely the white / blue / orange of photographed objects | Real objects carry their own color, so no palette is overlaid — the easiest and most advanced solution for a teaser |
| Semantic color | Red only, and only for "toppled / failed" | One meaning, not overused; no pile of red, green, blue, purple |
| Background | Pure white with a light grey table | Leaves red and the subject as the only focus, minimal visual noise |
| Double coding | Red box plus the text "Carton topples!" | Meaning does not depend on color alone, so it is colorblind-friendly (red is poorly discriminated by red-green colorblind readers, but the text backs it up) |
| Emotional symbols | 🙁 / 🤓 | Strong memorability but a stylistic risk; prepare a version without emoji in case a template renders them as boxes |

#### Five details worth copying

1. **Separate with dashed lines, not solid ones** — light, and they do not compete with the content.
2. **Single-variable rule**: apart from the method, camera, lighting, objects, and instruction are identical across the two rows.
3. **Three frames beat a thousand words**: demonstrate capability by showing that the action happened, not with adjectives.
4. **Position signals rank**: Ours is always at the bottom or right, where the eye ends.
5. **Four-part caption**: `Figure 1.` + bold subtitle + one summary sentence + `(left)/(top)/(bottom)` anchors + a statement of the advantage.

#### Four things I would change

1. Red appears only on the baseline side, with no positive color on the Ours side — the visual weight sits on failure. A very light thin border or a small check mark on the Ours row would help; avoid saturated green, which clashes with red.
2. The two row labels do not share a baseline (Vanilla centered, Ours left-shifted), which makes the layout look loose — left-align both labels to the same column.
3. When shrunk to thumbnail size, the three frames become indistinguishable — add a zoom-in circle around the contact point where the carton is pushed over.
4. The line-art human icon and the colored emoji do not share a style — pick one.

> **Tip**　One-line summary: **data figures use a palette; teasers use object color plus one semantic color.** These are different rules — many people overlay a pile of colored arrows and saturated labels on a photo, and the result is messier than the original image.

| Row | What it carries | What to copy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ① top　plane geometry | Proves the baseline works in an adjacent sub-domain | Give a "the baseline is fine" anchor first, so readers do not attribute the later failure to "a weak model" |
| ② middle　solid geometry | Pins the failure to exactly one sub-domain | Show the model's original answer (arccos0.9333) and let readers see the error themselves, rather than planting a red cross |
| ③ bottom　same problem + this paper | Succeeds on the same failure sample | Keep problem and answer format comparable; the only variable is the method |

#### Color audit: it relies on shape and layout, not color

| Dimension | What it does | Assessment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Background | Pure white plus light grey, no highlight blocks | Three dense rows leave no room for a busy background; it also survives greyscale printing |
| Verdict color | Only a green check and a red cross, and the shapes themselves carry the meaning | Color is only redundant coding, so colorblind readers read it through shape — red/green is the most dangerous pair, and shape rescues it here ✅ |
| Model icon | The same "stack of books" icon in all three rows, only the label differs | ⚠️ Cannot be told apart from the baseline at a glance; you must read the label to know the model changed |
| Emphasis | No colored arrows, no outlined highlights | Layout is organized by vertical alignment and whitespace — the most durable approach |
| Verdict column | Three symbols aligned in one vertical column | Lets readers scan vertically: the 2-wrong-1-right structure is visible within a second |

#### Five details worth copying

1. **Give the verdict symbols their own vertically aligned column** — the single most useful trick in the figure, more effective than any palette.
2. **Dashed lines only at task-domain switches**; no line between rows 2 and 3, which are both solid geometry — whitespace groups them (separators are a grouping tool, not row decoration).
3. **Fixed reading order within each row**: problem image → model → answer → verdict, identical in all three rows.
4. **Quote the answers**, imitating the model's own words, to stress "this is what it actually said" rather than the authors' paraphrase.
5. **Caption uses (top)/(middle)/(bottom)** and states in one sentence where it fails, where it succeeds, and what this paper does.

#### Five things I would change

1. **LaTeX source leaked into the figure**: the left panel still shows `\angleOAB=40°` and the problem statement contains `\theta` — check character by character before rendering; this is the most common accident when stitching screenshots.
2. **Ours and baseline share one icon**, distinguished only by label — at minimum change the color or add a thin border, otherwise readers know the model changed only after reading the label.
3. **Answer formats are not equivalent**: 50° (a value), arccos0.9333 (a four-decimal approximation), arctan(3/√205)° (an exact expression) — unify on the exact form, or tag approximations with `approx`, so readers can tell whether the value or the expression is wrong.
4. **The caption is missing a word**: "while showing performance in plane geometry" should be "while showing **strong** performance" — reviewers always read captions, so proofread them separately.
5. **The problem box is missing on the left of row 3**, inconsistent with the light grey boxes above, and the left column baselines are slightly misaligned across the three rows.

> **Tip**　**How Cases 1 and 2 divide the work:** Case 1 is a vertical comparison on the same task and wins on intuitiveness; Case 2 delimits the capability boundary by elimination and wins on rigor — it first blocks the most lethal objection, "your baseline is simply bad." **If your contribution is "better on one specific sub-problem", prefer the structure of Case 2.**

### 9.3　The Method Figure (Main Figure)

> **Tip**　Show the proposed method clearly, so readers understand the whole workflow and the core contribution.

Drawing workflow:

- Write one sentence stating what the figure expresses, and use it as the caption.
- List the elements that must appear and how they flow: input, output, core modules, and how intermediate information moves.
- Sketch the rough layout: layered top-down, left-right comparison, or a cyclic loop? Does the main flow run top-down or left-right? Where does the core contribution sit?
- Modularize and name: split the bulk into modules with clear inputs and outputs, and name each one. Names should be short and consistent with the main text.
- Unify the visual language: fix the colors, shapes, arrows, type, and icon style.

Core principles:

- Modularize the figure the way you modularize code — split the method into modules with clear inputs and outputs.
- Give every module a clear name consistent with the terminology in the main text.
- Arrows must express the flow explicitly: data flow, feedback, training order, and so on. Distinguish different meanings by style — solid arrows for the main flow, dashed for feedback, red for supervision signals, grey for auxiliary information.
- Use color and shape as auxiliary coding: one color per module type, different shapes for different functions, 3–5 colors at most, and no high-saturation or high-brightness colors.

#### Main results

Main results are usually best as a table, because a table handles precise comparison across many benchmarks, models, and metrics.

The design goal for a main-results table:

> **Tip**　Let readers find your method fastest and immediately see which baselines it beats.

Recommendations:

- Bold the best result.
- Underline the second best.
- Group rows that share a backbone or a setting into the same block.
- Group columns clearly by benchmark or metric.
- State the main conclusion directly in the caption.
- Do not leave the table sparse. If column or row spacing is very loose the table looks thin; adjust the layout or add information such as variances to fill it out.

### 9.5　Some Tips

#### 1. Color and palette tools

Whether for illustrations or result curves, the palette should be "clean and restrained, never harsh". Avoid high saturation — the cardinal sin in research figures is using pure saturated red, green, and blue. These tools give you a palette that matches academic taste in one click.

- Start from the PowerPoint default palette and adjust it; the figure below is built on a PowerPoint palette.

- ColorKit (<https://colorkit.co/palettes/>): a recommended palette site; generates gradients, extracts colors from images, and previews how a palette looks inside a chart.
- ColorBrewer 2.0 (<https://colorbrewer2.org/>): the classic standard for scientific visualization palettes, designed for maps and charts; supports "Colorblind safe" and "Print friendly" filters, ideal for trend plots and bar charts.
- SciVisColor (<https://sciviscolor.org/>): backed by the US National Science Foundation and others, designed specifically for scientific data visualization, with highly professional diverging and sequential palettes.
- Adobe Color (<https://color.adobe.com/>): build palettes by analogy, complementary, and other rules, or search "Scientific" and "Academic" in the Explore section to reuse palettes shared by designers worldwide.

#### 3. Vector icons and asset libraries (for overview and flow figures)

Overview and flow figures need many conceptual icons (servers, brains, data flow, comparison symbols). Using vector icons (SVG/EPS) of one consistent style is the key to "consistent type and consistent style".

- Flaticon (<https://www.flaticon.com/>): the largest free vector icon library. When drawing an overview figure, search for an "Icon Pack" so that devices, users, and arrows in the figure all share one design style (all line-art or all flat), avoiding a messy look.
- iconfont, Alibaba's vector icon library (<https://www.iconfont.cn/>): the strongest domestic icon library with excellent Chinese search. It hosts huge open-source icon packs by designers, and supports downloading SVG/PNG without login.
- The Noun Project (<https://thenounproject.com/>): extremely simple, highly abstract black-and-white symbol icons. If your overview figure aims for a refined minimalist look, this is an excellent source.
- BioRender (<https://biorender.com/>): a huge library of professional vector assets for cells, molecules, and lab equipment, assembled by drag and drop — the "PowerPoint" of biomedicine.

#### 4. Dedicated diagramming tools

For a method flow figure you need strong alignment, snapping, and connector routing, so arrows do not cross and directions stay uniform.

- Draw.io / Diagrams.net (<https://app.diagrams.net/>): a free, open-source, powerful diagramming tool with rich libraries for networks, cloud architecture, and general flowcharts. Available on the web and as a desktop app; exports lossless, re-editable SVG or PDF — an excellent replacement for Visio.
- ProcessOn (<https://www.processon.com/>): the leading domestic online diagramming tool, very quick to learn. Its template center has many ready-made research flowcharts and system architecture diagrams shared by academics that you can clone and edit.
- Lucidchart (<https://www.lucidchart.com/>): a very smooth collaborative diagramming tool whose smart connector routing and alignment are strong, suited to complex multi-module system diagrams.

#### 5. Code and visualization libraries for results figures

Trend plots and bar charts can be generated by a coding agent. When instructing the agent, name the following industry-standard libraries so the output already looks polished.

- Python — Seaborn & Matplotlib: the long-standing standard in research. Seaborn wraps Matplotlib with several academic themes (such as `whitegrid`) and has more modern default colors.
- Python — ProPlot (<https://proplot.readthedocs.io/>): a Matplotlib wrapper designed for publication-quality figures. It greatly simplifies subplot alignment, shared labels, and academic color palettes.

#### 6. Use AI to draft sketches (for overview-figure ideas)

For method flow figures, have AI draft a creative sketch first, then rebuild and refine it by hand.

- GPT-Image-2 / Midjourney / DALL-E 3: for teaser sketches, define the structure in the prompt (e.g., "A structured, 3-part scientific diagram from left to right, clean background, pastel color palette, vector style...") to get ideas for layout and visual guidance.

**💡 Writing tips**

> **Tip**　Hard rules for assets: **prefer vector formats.** When downloading from the sites above, always prefer .svg, .eps, or .pdf. Only vector art stays perfectly sharp with no missing glyphs when scaled up or shrunk to the actual two-column size of the paper. **Respect licensing.** When using commercial or open-source icon libraries for free, check the license (e.g., CC BY 3.0 requires attribution in the acknowledgements or caption, or choose fully open CC0 assets) to avoid copyright disputes.
