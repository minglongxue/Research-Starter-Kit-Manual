## 17 · A Guide to Attending Conferences

A conference is not only a stage for presenting results but also an opportunity to see the frontier, broaden your view, and build academic connections. For a graduate student the value is not in "listening to talks" or "presenting a paper" but in actively entering the academic community — learning to talk to peers and to meet more professors, students, and researchers. Stay open and proactive, and make full use of the opportunity.

### 17.1　Before the conference: arrive with goals

Browse the program in advance and focus on talks, workshops, and tutorials related to your direction. List a few talks you want to attend and researchers you want to meet — paper authors, PhD students in the same area, young faculty, members of relevant labs.

If you are presenting, prepare a concise, clear pitch in three lengths:

1. **30 seconds**: one sentence on what problem you work on.
2. **1 minute**: the research question, the method, and the main results.
3. **5 minutes**: technical details and follow-up thinking, for deeper conversations.

### 17.2　During the conference: meet people, do not stay with your own group

The most important rule: do not just sit and listen, and do not only move around with your own labmates. You can talk to your labmates any day — there is no reason to pay for travel to do it.

A conference is a rare chance for in-person contact. Many researchers whose names you have only seen in papers will be there. Take the initiative to talk with professors and students from other universities, labs, and directions.

Specifically:

#### Ask questions

If a talk interests you, ask in the Q&A. The question need not be profound, but it should be specific, sincere, and polite. Open with "great talk, I learned a lot — I have a quick question", for example:

- Why was a particular experimental setup designed this way?
- Does the method still hold in more complex scenarios?
- What problems in this direction are worth pursuing next?
- What are the main failure cases of the current method?

Asking also signals that you are interested in the area.

#### Talk to poster authors

The poster session is the part graduate students should value most. Compared with plenary talks, posters are freer and allow deeper discussion. Walk up to posters that interest you, ask the authors to walk you through the work, and share your own thoughts.

Start with simple questions, for example:

- "This is interesting — could you briefly explain your core idea?"
- "How does your method differ from XX?"
- "I have been looking at similar problems — what do you think is the biggest challenge in this direction?"

Do not worry about sounding unprofessional. Many collaborations, inspirations, and research ideas start from an ordinary poster conversation.

#### Introduce yourself

Learn to introduce yourself briefly. Prepare a natural template, for example:

> Hi, I'm a graduate student at the School of Intelligence Science and Technology, Nanjing University, working on LLM reasoning, agents, and neuro-symbolic learning. I just saw your talk / poster and it seems close to my work — I'd love to chat.

Keep it short, but make clear who you are, where you are from, what you work on, and why you want to talk.

#### Meet peers

Besides well-known scholars, pay attention to PhD and master's students from other universities and labs. Peers often discuss technical details more openly and may become long-term collaborators.

**Learn to exchange contact information proactively.**

Follow up politely if you have further questions. Many long-term collaborations in academia begin with a conversation at a conference during one's student years. At an international conference, send an email within 24 hours after talking — almost nobody does this, and it is very effective, for example:

```text
Subject: Great meeting you at [conference]
Hi [name],
It was great meeting you at [conference]! I really enjoyed our conversation about [the specific topic].
I've been thinking more about [a question]—[your follow-up thought, 1-2 sentences].
Looking forward to staying in touch!
Best,
[your name]
```

### 17.3　After the conference: turn conversations into assets

Do not let the takeaway stop at "I heard some talks and met some people". Organize the following promptly:

- **Papers worth reading closely**: work from talks or posters that inspired you.
- **People worth contacting**: the professors, students, and researchers you talked to.
- **New research ideas**: questions, methods, or experimental directions sparked at the conference.
- **Feedback on your own work**: if you presented, summarize the questions and suggestions you received.
- **A read on the field**: what the community is focused on, which directions are heating up, which problems remain open.

### 17.4　Mindset: open, confident, sincere, polite

Many students feel pressure at their first top-tier conference, thinking everyone else's work is stronger and not daring to ask or talk. In fact, academic exchange is not about showing that you already know everything — it is a process of learning and discussing together. Do not fear being stumped, and do not fear that your question is immature. Any question that comes from sincere thinking has value. What matters is stepping out, speaking up, and making contact.

Treat every conference as a chance to enter the academic community: listen more, ask more, talk more, meet more people, and let more people meet you. Be modest, polite, and generous with praise. Over time these conversations help you understand the field, find problems, and locate collaborators, and they matter for PhD applications, visiting positions, job hunting, and eventually running your own research.

### 17.5　A short checklist

- [ ] Get the contact information of at least 3 peers.
- [ ] Attend at least 1 talk related to your direction.
- [ ] Discuss at least 3 posters in depth.
- [ ] Note at least 3 papers to read closely afterwards.
- [ ] Do not only stay with your own labmates.
- [ ] Do not just take photos without thinking.
- [ ] Do not only listen to talks without talking to anyone.
- [ ] Do not feel embarrassed about exchanging contact information.
- [ ] Do not wait for others to approach you — initiating conversation is part of research training.

## 19 · Tips for Using AI in Research

A quick reference for AI coding tools, using Claude Code as the example:

### Common shortcuts

- `!`: run a shell command outside the session; the output enters the context.
- `@`: reference a file or path (with autocomplete).
- `#`: write a memory — append a line to CLAUDE.md.
- Newline without sending: Command + J or Option + Enter.
- Command + U: clear the current input line; Command + R: search prompt history.
- Command + V: paste a screenshot.
- Shift + Tab: cycle modes — normal (asks before each action) / plan mode (plan only, no file changes) / auto-accept edits (accept and run continuously).
- Write think / think hard / ultrathink in the prompt to raise the reasoning budget step by step.
- Esc: interrupt the current action immediately (without leaving the session); double Esc (= /rewind): roll back to an earlier message; /rewind can revert the conversation or code files.

### Context and memory

- **Context** is the current conversation — temporary; the longer it runs the fuller and slower it gets, and it disappears when you exit.
- **Memory (CLAUDE.md)** is project conventions written to a file — persistent; it survives clearing the context.
- /compact: compress into a summary and continue (use when the session slows down or nears its limit).
- /clear: clear everything and start a fresh session.
- /cost: show token usage for this session.
